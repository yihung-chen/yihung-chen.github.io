My research examines political parties, electoral politics, political behavior, and the political consequences of international economic and scientific conflicts.

Why Legislators Resign for Mayoral Races? A Study on Taiwan, 2008–2020

Political Parties · Electoral Politics

Abstract

Why do incumbent legislators seek executive office while retaining their legislative seats? This paper examines how party nomination rules shape legislators' decisions to pursue mayoral races in Taiwan. I argue that when electoral law permits legislators to retain their seats, party organizations play a central role in governing cross-level candidacies. By shaping nomination pathways, parties help ambitious politicians manage the risks and legitimacy of pursuing executive office while holding legislative office.

When the Signal Breaks: Origin-Country Elite Conflict and Vaccine Acceptance in Taiwan

Political Behavior · Public Opinion

Abstract

Why do people prefer vaccines developed in some countries over others, and can this preference be undermined by political conflict? This paper examines whether disputes among political elites in a vaccine's country of origin weaken the perceived credibility of its vaccines. Using survey experiments in Taiwan, I investigate how negative information and contested debates over mRNA vaccine safety shape public support for vaccine procurement and vaccination willingness.

Backlash to Weaponized Non-Tariff Trade Barriers

Political Economy · Foreign Economic Coercion

Abstract

How does foreign economic coercion shape domestic political behavior? This paper examines the political consequences of China's restrictions on Taiwanese agricultural exports, focusing on whether economically targeted communities respond to trade pressure through changes in electoral support.

Foreign Economic Coercion and Domestic Coalition of Voluntary Regulatory Harmonization: The Case of Sugar Apple Export from Taiwan to China

Political Economy · Trade and Regulation

Abstract

Why does foreign economic coercion sometimes induce domestic actors to voluntarily harmonize their regulatory practices with the demands of a coercing state? This paper argues that such coalitions are more likely to emerge where economic dependence is geographically concentrated, creating local interests that favor regulatory accommodation. The study examines Taiwan's sugar apple exports to China.

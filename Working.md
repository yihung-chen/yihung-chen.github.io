## 		A Party-Made Ladder: Intra-Party Nomination Rules and the Institutionalization of Level-Hopping

*Political Parties · Electoral Politics*

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

When legislators can run for executive office without giving up their seats, what restrains cross-level ambition, and why do voters accept it? This article argues that, where parties control nominations, intra-party rules both ration access to higher office and legitimate those who obtain it. Because a mid-term candidacy shifts its risks from the individual to the party, the party decides who may climb, where, and by which route. Evidence from Taiwan, combining original data on all legislators elected 2008-2020, a survey experiment, and elite interviews, supports this argument. Legislators seek executive office almost only in races their party leaves open, party rules shape where they compete in primaries, and those with a strong existing base are drafted. Voters judge such moves through partisanship: co-partisans tolerate them, out-partisans condemn them, and primaries raise support only among co-partisans. Accountability for mid-term departures thus shifts from constituents to parties.

## Ambition, Horizontal Coordination, and the Reverse Coattail Effect: Evidence from Taiwan

*Political Behavior · Electoral Politics*

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

How do multiple ambitious local elites within the same party coordinate—or fail to coordinate—when mobilizing for a higher-tier objective? The reverse coattail literature has developed largely within single-member district contexts, leaving the horizontal interdependence among co-partisans in multi-member districts underexplored. This study argues that whether multiple ambitious local elites amplify or undermine mobilization depends on the excludability of the party’s electoral objective: non-excludable defensive goals invite free-riding, whereas excludable offensive prizes fuel a tournament that escalates with peer density. Using the 2025 Taiwan legislative recall as a quasi-experiment, propensity score matching reveals a behavioral asymmetry. Defending star councilors produce a protective effect that fails to scale under multi-agent crowding, while challenging star agents suppress the same margin, amplifying as more challengers compete. The study offers a portable identification strategy for contexts combining multi-member lower-tier institutions, single-member upper-tier objectives, and non-concurrent electoral cycles.

## When the Signal Breaks: Origin-Country Elite Conflict and Vaccine Acceptance in Taiwan
(with Jason Kuo)

*Political Behavior · Public Opinion*

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

When a country’s own political leadership publicly contests the credibility of its pharmaceutical exports, does the country-of-origin (COO) premium that makes those products internationally preferred erode? Existing research consistently finds that vaccine origin countries shape public willingness to receive vaccination, with American-origin vaccines occupying a particularly favorable position in public evaluations. Yet this literature rests on an unexamined assumption that origin-country credibility is stable and internally consistent. The political developments of early 2025 placed this assumption under direct pressure. We examine how U.S. elite conflict over mRNA technology affects Taiwanese support and vaccination intention for U.S.-developed mRNA vaccines, arguing that political conflict within the origin country attenuates the COO premium and introduces a previously overlooked source of vaccination hesitancy among foreign publics.
Across two pre-registered survey experiments conducted in Taiwan, we find that both an administrative opposition message and a contested debate juxtaposing opposing elite views significantly reduced procurement support and vaccination intention, whereas affirming information had only a marginal effect. Causal mediation analyses reveal distinct mechanisms. Administrative opposition operated through perceived protection, while contested debate operated through perceived safety. These findings show the COO premium is politically contingent; elite conflict in the origin country can erode an advantage long treated as fixed, translating domestic disputes into consequences for global health attitudes.

</details>

## Backlash to Weaponized Non-Tariff Trade Barriers: Evidence from the Chinese Mango Ban in the 2024 Taiwan Presidential Election
(with Jason Kuo)

*Political Behavior · Public Opinion*

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

On August 21, 2023, the mainland Chinese government unilaterally declared a ban on the sale of Taiwanese mangoes to mainland China, citing quarantine non-compliance as the reason. The incumbent Democratic Progressive Party (DDP) believed that this incident was a malicious attempt by the mainland Chinese government to intervene in Taiwan's presidential election through foreign economic coercion. However, the existing literature has not assessed the electoral impact of the weaponized non-tariff barriers (NTBs) to trade as a Chinese coercive foreign economic policy intervention in a small open economy, such as Taiwan. To fill this gap, this study systematically collected administrative data from 368 townships in Taiwan and used a difference-in-differences research design to estimate the average causal effect of the Chinese mango ban as a trade coercion through NTBs on the 2024 Taiwanese presidential election. This study found that the Chinese mango ban significantly increased political support for the incumbent DPP vote share in the presidential election by 2.6 percentage points in the nine southern townships materialistically hurt by the ban. In other words, the Chinese use of NTBs as a foreign policy tool of economic coercion backfired, causing a political backlash among citizens residing in the neighborhoods of the coerced Taiwanese mango farmers. This study also attributed the pattern to the strategic prevention of the anticipated economic pain of export diversification by the ruling DPP government. Despite the increasing use of foreign trade policy for economic coercion by China and the other great powers around the world in recent years, this study reveals serious limitations of foreign trade coercion to intervene in democratic elections.

## From Rally to Rupture: Trump’s Tariffs in Taiwan
(with Jason Kuo)

*Political Behavior · Public Opinion*

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

How does explicit economic pressure by a security patron affect domestic politics in a dependent ally? We examine Taiwan’s response to Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs and the ensuing U.S.–Taiwan Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART). Although sudden, dramatic, international events are expected to induce rally-around-the-flag effects, we argue that economic pressure from a security patron need not produce a rally but instead deepens preexisting partisan cleavages by weakening public confidence in the incumbent and favorability toward the patron among opposition but not ruling party supporters. We conduct two original surveys to test this claim. A survey, fielded after the first reduction in the negotiated tariffs in August 2025, finds no rally. Instead, respondents viewed the tariffs as harmful to the national economy, were dissatisfied with the trade negotiations, and expressed limited confidence in the government.
Responses also significantly diverged by partisan camp. We then exploit the ART’s unexpected January 2026 announcement during our follow-up survey to identify causal effects of the ART concession. Among opposition supporters, U.S. favorability fell by 16 percent of its range immediately after the announcement; yet ruling-party supporters showed no significant change. Aggregated interrupted time-series analyses of the ART concession further show that this erosion in U.S. favorability among opposition supporters remained stable throughout the observed post-announcement period. Our results broadly suggest that economic pressure from a security patron, the U.S. in this case, cannot rally a dependent ally’s public behind its incumbent. More generally, it can deepen domestic partisan divisions and erode cross-partisan support for the security patron.
</details>
